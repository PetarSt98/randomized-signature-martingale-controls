# Battery scenario

The physical target is energy that can actually be delivered when a controller requests 4 MW for 15 minutes. It is a short, high-power block on a 2 MWh nominal pack. The battery starts discharging at the quote, delivers 4 MW while eligible, and stops for the rest of the block at the first low-voltage cutoff. The maximum is 1 MWh. Even a brief first delivery counts. This exercise assumes a constant-power controller with a trip. A power-derating controller, heterogeneous pack with weak cells or an operator with advance knowledge of a battery-management-system discharge forecast would change the problem.

## Source and independent implementation

The model in physics.py implements the reduced electrochemical equations of Matthew Daigle and Chetan Kulkarni, *Electrochemistry-based Battery Modeling for Prognostics* (2013), equations 10–20 and 25–37, with the 18650-cell coefficients from Tables 1–2. The reference compares the cell model with experimental discharge curves. It did not validate the installation, scenario distribution, contract, hedging results, or estimator used here. This implementation uses NumPy and does not call NASA ProgPy.

- https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140009120/downloads/20140009120.pdf
- https://doi.org/10.36001/phmconf.2013.v5i1.2252

For context, Monzio Compagnoni and coauthors used a related NASA battery simulator to compare voltage predictions. They measured voltage-error performance. The contract payoff and all contract experiments in this package are new simulations. See https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00384 .

## State, health uncertainty and measurements

Each scenario starts from electrochemical equilibrium with a nominal state of charge uniformly distributed between 25% and 90%. The simulation treats the representative cells in one equivalent pack as identical. It scales the number of cells to 2 MWh using 3.7 V nominal voltage and 2.2 Ah per cell. There is no inverter loss, pack imbalance, intermittent outage, or degradation within an episode.

Nominal cell charge comes from 0.6 × 13,200 coulombs. The surface and bulk compartment volumes are 2e−6 and 2e−5 cubic metres. The equilibrium voltage uses the published Redlich–Kister expansion. The ohmic drop and the two surface activation drops use published time constants of 10 and 90 seconds. Activation follows Butler–Volmer kinetics. Because the source's electrode transport coefficients and volumes are matched, the implementation uses the total negative electrode charge, its surface departure from equilibrium, the ohmic drop and the sum of both activation drops. The positive mole fraction is one minus the negative mole fraction.

The pack temperature is uniform between 15 and 25 °C across scenarios, observed and held constant over each episode. It enters the equilibrium and activation equations. The independent health draws, fixed within an episode, multiply the paper's cell ohmic resistance (0.085 ohms) by a uniform factor between 1 and 2 and its diffusion coefficient D (7e6) by a uniform factor between 0.8 and 2. In this convention larger D means slower diffusion. These are analyst-selected cross-scenario uncertainty ranges. The paper provides the nominal parameters and physical mechanism. They do not constitute an empirical distribution of electricity-storage installations. The nameplate capacity is the same in every episode.

The trader receives 49 readings over 48 one-minute intervals. Every reading includes the current state of charge, the applied current over the preceding minute in C-rate, terminal cell voltage with independent 5 mV Gaussian measurement noise, and measured temperature. SOC, temperature and applied current are observed without noise. The initial reading is taken at open circuit. Applied charging/discharging current is a sequence of random pulses, uniform from −1C to +1C, redrawn at probability 0.22 per minute. A positive current discharges the pack. An operating policy limits each step to 12–92% SOC. Any step that would end above 4.2 V when charging or below 3 V when discharging is replaced by one minute of rest. These checks use noiseless voltage inside the physical controller. The models receive the actual applied current and noisy voltage. The protocol supplies no true resistance, diffusion coefficient, surface concentration, or management-system availability forecast to the history-based methods.

Future deliverability is simulated deterministically conditional on the full physical state, which is unavailable to the fitted history-based models. The simulation holds temperature and health at their episode values and requests constant power, stopping irreversibly for that block when terminal voltage reaches 3.0 V or SOC reaches zero. The conservative 3.0 V operational threshold is a modeling assumption. The allotted energy is the elapsed fraction of the 15-minute call times 1 MWh. The constant-power solver adjusts cell current every five seconds and linearly interpolates cutoff inside the last step. For preceding one-minute intervals, diffusion and ohmic states use the exact constant-current transition, and surface kinetics use the average of the starting and ending surface mole fractions.

## Numerical checks and limitations

Run `python tests/test_battery_delivery.py` from the repository root. Tests check charge conservation against observed current, physical delivery bounds, reproducibility, and the equilibrium-polynomial formula. An independent seven-state SciPy ODE solution for a 600-second, 1.4 A constant discharge at 292 K agreed with the simplified state update to 0.000012 V. On 3,000 independent QA histories, halving repeatedly to a one-second delivery step gave a 0.00159 MWh RMSE difference from five-second targets, maximum 0.00288 MWh.

On a separate set of 5,000 QA histories (seed 901), mean deliverability was 0.243 MWh, the median was 0.089 MWh, and the fifth and 95th percentiles were 0.018 and 0.895 MWh. About 2.6% delivered the whole 1 MWh. No history was removed. The deliberately demanding 2C nameplate request, resistance variation, and voltage cutoff make curtailed delivery common. This distribution should be interpreted as a high-power availability stress scenario, and should not be used as a forecast of utilization or curtailment at an actual battery plant.
