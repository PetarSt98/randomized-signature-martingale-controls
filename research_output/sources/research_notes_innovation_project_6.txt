Updated Research Note
Randomized-Signature Reservoirs
for Partial-Information Hedging
Causal State Representations for Observed-Filtration GKW Hedging
Petar Stojković Updated research note, ETH Zurich June 2026
Problem and Research Question
The trader does not observe the full simulator state. The discounted tradable prices are observed, together with a noisy signal
about volatility, but the latent volatility itself is hidden. A hedge must therefore be chosen from the observed history only. For
a path-dependent payoff, this history can matter: the current price and current signal may not contain enough information
for a good hedge. The mathematical target is the best hedge achievable from the observed filtration. In the formulation
below, this is the traded-gain component in the partial-information Galtchouk–Kunita–Watanabe decomposition.
The question is how to summarize the observed path in a finite-dimensional state useful for hedging. A classical filter gives
a model-based estimate of the hidden volatility. Finite signatures give systematic path features, but their dimension grows
quickly. A Transformer gives a flexible trainable sequence model, but uses non-convex training. A randomized-signature
reservoir gives another compromise: the reservoir dynamics are sampled once, and only a linear or affine hedge readout is
trained.
Research question: Can randomized-signature reservoirs use the observed history of prices and noisy volatility signals
to build a useful state for G-predictable quadratic hedging? The empirical test is how much of the hedging-risk gap
between raw observed features and the fitted true-state benchmark they close, and how that compares with a classical
filter, finite signatures, and a causal Transformer.
Central claim and novelty boundary. The project studies the effect of the information constraint on hedging. The hedge
is allowed to use only the observed filtration G, not the hidden volatility or future information. The current contribution
is the controlled comparison of randomized-signature reservoir states as causal features for the observed-filtration GKW
hedge.
The project does not claim a new GKW decomposition, a new filtering method, a new signature method, or a new
deep-hedging framework. The point is narrower: to compare reservoir states with raw features, a classical filter, finite
signatures, and a causal Transformer under the same observed-information constraint. The reservoir is called filter-like only
because it compresses the observed history causally; classical filters remain explicit benchmarks.
Preliminary partial-information hedging evidence. With 10,000/3,000/10,000 train/validation/test paths, η =
0.02, and validation-only model selection, the reservoir reduced one-asset Heston Asian hedging RMSE from 2.4037 for
raw observed features to 1.0167 on the N = 252 grid. The corresponding RMSEs were 1.1026 for finite signatures,
2.2579 for the implemented particle-filter quadratic hedge, 0.8102 for the causal Transformer, and 0.7921 for the fitted
true-state Transformer benchmark. The reservoir closed 92.1% of the empirical raw-to-fitted-true-state MSE gap.
The same experiment was also run for a three-asset Heston basket Asian payoff. There, RMSE was 2.0981 for raw
observed features, 1.0573 for the reservoir, 1.2486 for finite signatures, 0.8684 for the Transformer, and 0.8496 for its
fitted true-state benchmark. The reservoir closed 89.2% of the empirical gap. The same method ordering and nearly
identical gap closures were also obtained on the N = 52 grid.
Paired test-path bootstrap intervals supported lower reservoir MSE than raw features and finite signatures in both
experiments, and lower reservoir MSE than the implemented particle-filter hedge in the one-asset experiment. The
Transformer achieved lower MSE than the reservoir. These are single-seed preliminary results, conditional on the fitted
models and representation draws. The observation-noise sweep and multi-seed analysis remain the final validation steps.
Completed first iteration: martingale-control pricing. The project started from martingale control variates
for path-dependent Monte Carlo pricing using randomized-signature reservoirs. This first iteration established the
adapted-readout construction, sample-split unbiasedness, and the comparison with finite-signature controls. In preliminary
N = 252 benchmarks, reservoir readouts achieved lower held-out residual variance than matched finite-signature controls
across Black–Scholes, Heston, multi-asset Heston, and commodity-curve pricing examples. These results are kept as appendix
evidence for the adapted-readout machinery, but they are not the main evidence for the partial-information hedging claim.

Gap and novelty boundary. Several neighbouring literatures are already active. Classical partial-information and
restricted-information quadratic hedging provide the L2 projection backbone. Signature-based hedging, random-feature hedging,
deep hedging, filtering-based hedging, and machine-learning hedging with non-tradable factors are also well studied. In particular,
Neufeld–Schmocker are close prior art for quadratic hedging with iterated-integral random features and trained linear readouts, while
Fécamp–Mikael–Warin are close prior art for machine-learning hedging with non-tradable factors.
The information and trading restriction is classical. What is specific here is the comparison of causal state representations under that
restriction. The trader observes
Gtk = σ(Si, Yi : 0 ≤ i ≤ k),
where Y is informative but not traded, and hedge gains are generated only from ∆S(cid:101)k+1 . Within this restriction, the note evaluates
randomized-signature reservoir states built causally from (S, Y ) against raw features, a classical filter, finite signatures, and a causal
Transformer.
The claim is therefore narrow. This is not a new GKW theorem, not a new filtering method, not generic signature hedging, not
readout-only random-feature hedging, and not generic deep hedging. The contribution is the controlled comparison of reservoir states
for observed-filtration quadratic hedging with a noisy non-traded signal.
Contributions
• Observed-filtration projection view. The hedging problem is written as an L2 projection onto terminal traded gains. This
gives three nested comparison classes: state-information strategies, observed-filtration strategies, and the finite feature classes used
in the experiments. This separates the loss from not observing V from the finite-representation restriction.
• Reservoir hedge class. The project builds causal reservoir states from the observed price and signal history, and then trains
only a linear or affine hedge readout. The resulting hedge is G-predictable by construction. Classical filters, finite observed-path
signatures, and a causal Transformer are used as comparators, so that the reservoir is tested against model-based filtering, signature
features, and a fully trained sequence model.
• Empirical hedging comparison. The main quantity is out-of-sample quadratic hedging risk as the observation noise changes.
The experiments measure how much each method improves on raw observed features and how close it gets to the fitted true-state
benchmark. The comparison reports feature dimension and computational cost; the remaining analysis adds path-budget sensitivity
and stability across simulation and representation seeds.
Completed foundation. The martingale-control pricing study is supporting appendix evidence, not a separate main contribution.
Not claimed: this is not a new theory of GKW decompositions, partial-information hedging, nonlinear filtering, signatures, randomized
signatures, Transformers, martingale control variates, or sample splitting. The contribution is the filtration-constrained use and
evaluation of randomized-signature states for quadratic hedging.

make clear which strategies are admissible, what the population L2 hedge is, and how the experiments separate information loss from
finite-representation error. The pricing-control arguments from the first iteration are treated as appendix material.

then adds its own causal state.

Evaluation protocol. All model choices are made on validation paths only and then fixed before test evaluation. The current results
are single-seed results. The remaining robustness analysis will repeat the experiments across simulation seeds and, where relevant,
reservoir draws and Transformer initializations. The final comparison will report paired uncertainty intervals, feature dimension, fitted
parameter count, training time, evaluation time, memory use, and stability across path budgets.
Remaining ablations. The remaining ablations vary the observation-noise level η, reservoir dimension D, time-grid size N, signature
truncation level L, ridge parameter λ, reservoir input channels, and Transformer window length w. A negative result is also useful: if
raw features, the particle filter, finite signatures, or the Transformer retain hedge-relevant information better than the reservoir, this
should be reported rather than hidden.
Limitations and Failure Modes
The preliminary results are encouraging, but they are not final benchmark claims. The reservoir improves on raw features, finite
signatures, and the implemented particle-filter quadratic hedge, but it does not outperform the causal Transformer. The main empirical
question is therefore the risk–complexity trade-off, and how this trade-off changes with observation noise.
The fitted true-state benchmark is not the population state-information optimum. Gap closure can also be unstable when the
raw-to-true-state denominator is small. For this reason, the component risks and paired uncertainty intervals remain the main reported
quantities.
The results may depend on reservoir dimension and draw, input scaling, ridge regularization, simulation seed, and Transformer
initialization or attention window. The current pricing and hedging results are single-seed preliminary evidence. Final conclusions
require repeated simulation seeds and representation seeds.
The information constraint is strict. Hidden Brownian shocks and latent variance may be used for diagnostics or for the fitted true-state
benchmark, but not by an admissible G-predictable hedge. Wealth gains must be generated only by traded increments. The experiment
is also discrete-observation; it should not be read as continuous observation of the full price path.
